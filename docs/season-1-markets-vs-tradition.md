# Season 1 research map: markets versus traditional goods

This document is a research slate, not a list of settled conclusions. Every episode must satisfy the [editorial evidence standard](./editorial-evidence-standard.md) before a script can be approved.

The season's central thesis is deliberately narrower than “capitalism destroys tradition”:

> Markets are powerful coordination systems, but prices and profits do not automatically encode every moral good. Where buyers lack information or choice, vulnerable people cannot bargain effectively, attention is monetized, ownership is concentrated, or costs are shifted onto families and communities, commercial incentives can conflict with traditional values. Markets can also create prosperity, autonomy, innovation, and institutions that protect those same values. The episode must identify which mechanism is actually operating.

## 1. Medicine: the patient or the revenue unit?

**Traditional good:** healing as a vocation, mercy, continuity of care, and the equal dignity of the sick.

**Market mechanism:** fee-for-service reimbursement, market consolidation, pharmaceutical exclusivity, insurer utilization rules, or acquisition structures can reward revenue, throughput, or financial extraction rather than prevention and continuity.

**Evidence-ready claim:** federal competition and health agencies have investigated whether certain private-equity and corporate transactions generate profits while threatening quality, affordability, worker safety, or access. That establishes a credible policy concern, not that every for-profit provider delivers worse care.

**Counterargument:** private capital can expand facilities, fund research, improve logistics, and scale treatments. Public and nonprofit systems also ration care and can be inefficient.

**Grade:** B until an episode selects a defined payment mechanism, provider type, outcome, and study design.

**Do not say:** “For-profit medicine only makes money and never helps people.”

## 2. Contract prisons: when occupancy becomes a revenue condition

**Traditional good:** proportionate punishment, public safety, rehabilitation, humane treatment, and accountable state power.

**Market mechanism:** a contractor is paid to operate incarceration capacity. Contract design, staffing costs, occupancy provisions, monitoring quality, and political activity must each be examined separately.

**Evidence-ready claim:** a DOJ Inspector General comparison found more safety and security incidents per capita in most measured categories at the contract prisons it reviewed and found deficiencies in federal monitoring.

**Counterargument:** contracting can provide capacity and specialization; observed differences may reflect population, contract, staffing, or oversight choices rather than profit status alone.

**Grade:** A for the audit's scoped findings; C for a general claim that private-prison companies caused harsher criminal laws unless direct lobbying and legislative evidence is added.

**Do not say:** “The audit proves private prisons created mass incarceration.”

## 3. Justice for sale: unequal access to counsel

**Traditional good:** equality before the law and adjudication on the merits.

**Market mechanism:** expert counsel, investigation, discovery, delay, and procedural capacity are expensive. Resource asymmetry can affect who can assert rights effectively even when the written law is formally equal.

**Evidence-ready claim:** the Legal Services Corporation's national study reported that low-income Americans received no or insufficient legal help for most substantial civil legal problems, with cost a major barrier.

**Counterargument:** privately retained counsel protects individuals from state power, specialization improves legal quality, and public defense/legal aid can coexist with a private bar.

**Grade:** A for the documented civil-justice gap; B for claims about procedural advantage; case-specific evidence required for criminal defendants or corporate litigation.

**Do not say:** “For-profit lawyers make wealthy criminals above the law.”

## 4. Universities, revenue, and women's education

**Traditional good:** education as moral and civic formation, family continuity, and truth-seeking rather than credential sales.

**Possible market mechanism:** tuition dependence and institutional growth can shape recruitment, amenities, pricing, and credential expansion.

**Status of the proposed claim:** “Universities wanted daughters enrolled so their fathers would pay” is not supported as a general historical explanation and is not publishable in that form.

**Researchable replacement:** choose a defined university or sector and investigate its budgets, recruitment materials, board records, admissions expansion, and labor-market positioning. Separate the history of women's educational opportunity from criticism of modern tuition and credential incentives.

**Counterargument:** expanded education increased women's knowledge, agency, professional options, and civic participation; those gains cannot be reduced to university revenue motives.

**Grade:** C as a general inquiry; institution-specific evidence could raise individual claims to A or B.

## 5. Women's work, wages, and the family economy

**Traditional good:** family formation, caregiving, household sovereignty, and the ability to choose paid work or home-centered work without economic coercion.

**Possible market mechanism:** employers may seek lower-cost labor, and a larger labor supply can affect bargaining conditions. The relevant effects vary by industry, period, skill, law, and household structure.

**Evidence-ready claim:** the historical transformation of women's employment was multi-causal and included education, changing expectations and identity, fertility, technology, law, occupational change, and employer demand. Industry-specific wage discrimination or recruitment can be documented, but a coordinated plan by “the capitalist class” is not established.

**Counterargument:** paid employment and education expanded women's autonomy, household resilience, bargaining power, and talent available to society. A one-earner household also depended on class, race, and historical context and was never universal.

**Grade:** B for carefully scoped labor-market mechanisms; D for the universal coordination claim.

**Better question:** why did productivity growth not translate into enough household purchasing power and time sovereignty for more families to choose a single earner?

## 6. Attention markets and degrading media

**Traditional good:** modesty, childhood innocence, truthful speech, public decency, and culture that cultivates rather than merely stimulates.

**Market mechanism:** advertising, subscriptions, recommendation systems, and engagement metrics reward attention. Sensationalism, outrage, violence, and sexual content can be inexpensive ways to acquire it.

**Evidence-ready claim:** the FTC found that major social-media and video-streaming firms collected and monetized extensive personal data and that many business models incentivized mass data collection, with inadequate protections for children and teens.

**Counterargument:** commercial media also finances art, education, minority viewpoints, and access to information; audiences exercise agency, and censorship can threaten legitimate expression.

**Grade:** A for identified data/advertising practices; B for the broader argument that engagement incentives degrade culture; specific content-effect claims require separate research.

## 7. Pornography as a commercial attention product

**Traditional good:** sexual fidelity, modesty, privacy, human dignity, and protection from exploitation.

**Market mechanism:** subscriptions, advertising, data collection, affiliate marketing, creator platforms, and frictionless distribution monetize sexual attention at enormous scale.

**Research requirements:** distinguish consensual adult production from trafficking or coercion; verify revenue claims; separate evidence about use patterns, relationships, mental health, labor conditions, and minors; and avoid treating association as causation.

**Counterargument:** adults assert interests in autonomy and expression; prohibition can drive activity underground and worsen safety; regulation must distinguish consensual conduct from abuse.

**Grade:** B for commodification and platform incentives; individual harm claims range from B to D depending on study quality.

## 8. Housing as a portfolio rather than a home

**Traditional good:** stable households, rooted neighborhoods, intergenerational continuity, and broad property ownership.

**Market mechanism:** institutional buyers can value houses as yield-producing assets, compete with owner-occupants using cash and scale, and centralize rent-setting and property management.

**Evidence-ready claim:** HUD summarized evidence that institutional and other large corporate investors held a growing share of single-family homes in selected markets, removed units from individual-buyer supply, and placed upward pressure on prices and rents, with purchases geographically concentrated.

**Counterargument:** institutional landlords can add rental supply, renovate neglected properties, and provide professionally managed housing; zoning and construction constraints may be more important drivers of scarcity.

**Grade:** B nationally; A only for the places and periods supported by the selected dataset.

## 9. Surveillance capitalism and the private home

**Traditional good:** the home as a private sanctuary, freedom of conscience, and the right not to have intimate behavior converted into a product.

**Market mechanism:** targeted advertising and automated optimization reward collection, retention, combination, and inference from personal data.

**Evidence-ready claim:** the FTC reported extensive collection and monetization by major social and streaming platforms, inadequate minimization and retention practices, broad sharing, weak user control, and particular concerns for minors.

**Counterargument:** data-funded services can be inexpensive or free at the point of use, personalization can be useful, and privacy-preserving advertising models are possible.

**Grade:** A for the FTC's scoped findings; B for the normative conclusion that the model violates the sanctity of private life.

## 10. Marketing around parents and directly to children

**Traditional good:** parental authority, childhood innocence, and formation protected from manipulative commercial pressure.

**Market mechanism:** brands seek early loyalty and purchasing influence; games, influencers, social video, and immersive environments can blur entertainment and advertising.

**Evidence-ready claim:** FTC staff warned that younger children often cannot reliably distinguish blurred advertising from surrounding content and identified risks from influencer, game, and targeted formats.

**Counterargument:** advertising can fund children's media and inform families about products; parental controls and clear disclosures can reduce some risks.

**Grade:** A for documented format and regulatory findings; B for broader cultural effects.

## 11. Planned obsolescence, repair restrictions, and stewardship

**Traditional good:** thrift, craftsmanship, maintenance, inheritance, and responsible use of material goods.

**Market mechanism:** replacement sales, locked parts, proprietary tools, software restrictions, and warranty conditions can make repair harder or more expensive.

**Evidence-ready claim:** the FTC has brought enforcement actions and issued warnings over warranty terms that restricted independent repair or implied that third-party parts and service would void coverage.

**Counterargument:** integrated repair networks can protect safety, cybersecurity, quality, and intellectual property; rapid innovation is not always deliberate obsolescence.

**Grade:** A for specific warranty practices; B for an industry-wide “disposable culture” interpretation; direct design evidence is required before calling a product deliberately short-lived.

## 12. Just-in-time labor versus family time

**Traditional good:** predictable family meals, worship, caregiving, civic participation, and a shared weekly rhythm.

**Market mechanism:** variable and on-call scheduling lets employers match labor costs closely to demand, shifting uncertainty onto hourly workers.

**Evidence-ready claim:** the ILO describes unstable schedules as attractive to employers for flexibility while imposing worker costs through income instability and difficulty coordinating non-market time. Its broader working-time review links undesired long hours and schedule arrangements to poorer work-life balance.

**Counterargument:** some workers value flexibility, and better forecasting can preserve jobs during volatile demand; schedule rules can increase employer costs or reduce available hours.

**Grade:** A for the documented tradeoff; B for the conclusion that it erodes family or religious life in a particular population.

## 13. Algorithmic management and human dignity at work

**Traditional good:** personal judgment, reciprocal obligations, fair supervision, and work as more than a sequence of optimized motions.

**Market mechanism:** software can allocate tasks, evaluate pace, predict staffing, discipline workers, and monitor behavior at low marginal cost.

**Evidence-ready claim:** ILO/JRC case studies report efficiency and process-simplification benefits alongside potential deterioration in job quality and intrusive worker surveillance.

**Counterargument:** algorithms can reduce favoritism, improve safety and coordination, and make performance standards more consistent when transparent and appealable.

**Grade:** A for the scoped case studies; B for wider cultural conclusions.

## 14. Elder care as a metric-managed service

**Traditional good:** filial duty, dignity in old age, truthful care records, and continuity of relationships.

**Market mechanism:** reimbursement, staffing expense, ownership structure, quality ratings, and occupancy can create pressure to minimize costs or understate adverse events.

**Evidence-ready claim:** a 2025 HHS Inspector General review found that nursing homes failed to report 43 percent of the examined falls involving major injury and hospitalization; for-profit, chain, and larger homes failed most often in that review. The report identifies a reporting disincentive but does not establish that ownership alone caused every failure.

**Counterargument:** professional facilities provide complex care that dispersed families often cannot safely deliver; nonprofit and public homes can also fail.

**Grade:** A for the audit findings; B for ownership-based causal interpretation.

## 15. Grief under time pressure: the funeral market

**Traditional good:** burial and mourning as sacred family and community duties rather than ordinary retail transactions.

**Market mechanism:** grieving consumers make urgent, emotionally charged purchases with limited experience and asymmetric information, creating opportunities for bundling and opaque pricing.

**Evidence-ready claim:** the FTC Funeral Rule requires itemized pricing, permits consumers to select only desired goods and services, and prohibits certain forced bundles and misrepresentations. Repeated enforcement history shows that the risk is concrete, although the rule itself is not proof that every provider exploits families.

**Counterargument:** funeral professionals perform difficult skilled work, coordinate legal and logistical obligations, and help families honor the dead; profit does not negate vocation.

**Grade:** A for rule requirements and cited enforcement; B for the broader commercialization thesis.

## 16. Ultra-processed food, convenience, and temperance

**Traditional good:** temperance, shared meals, bodily stewardship, local food knowledge, and hospitality.

**Market mechanism:** shelf life, scale, formulation, portability, branding, and repeat purchase can reward products that are easy to consume quickly and frequently.

**Evidence-ready claim:** a small, tightly controlled NIH randomized crossover trial of 20 adults found that participants ate roughly 500 more calories per day and gained weight on the ultra-processed diet compared with the minimally processed diet, despite matched presented calories and macronutrients. The study did not identify which feature of processing caused the effect or establish every long-term population outcome.

**Counterargument:** processing improves safety, affordability, consistency, nutrition fortification, and access; minimally processed diets can require more money and preparation time.

**Grade:** A for the trial's measured short-term outcome; B or C for claims about commercial intent and population-wide cultural effects.

## 17. Sacred time converted into continuous commerce

**Traditional good:** Sabbath or a common day of rest, family gatherings, worship, and limits on commercial demands.

**Possible market mechanism:** extended retail hours and continuous digital commerce reward availability whenever demand exists and make synchronized rest costly for individual firms.

**Research requirements:** document the history of local closing laws, sector-specific schedule changes, worker preferences, religious participation, and economic effects. Avoid romanticizing a past in which many agricultural, domestic, medical, transport, and service workers still labored on nominal rest days.

**Counterargument:** varied schedules serve pluralistic religious observance, emergency needs, consumer convenience, and worker preferences; legal closing rules can privilege one tradition.

**Grade:** C until the episode selects a place, period, and dataset.

## 18. Global scale versus local rootedness

**Traditional good:** local craftsmanship, national resilience, reciprocal employer-community obligations, and distinctive regional culture.

**Possible market mechanism:** firms optimize across wage, tax, logistics, and regulatory differences; standardized chains and global procurement can displace local production and homogenize commercial landscapes.

**Research requirements:** use a concrete industry or town. Measure employment, prices, quality, resilience, consumer welfare, and cultural effects. Do not assume every import, migrant worker, multinational firm, or standardized product has the same effect.

**Counterargument:** trade lowers prices, expands choice, raises incomes, and connects communities; local incumbents can be inefficient or exclusionary.

**Grade:** B for a well-supported case study; C as a universal claim.

## Reserve investigations

### Gambling-like monetization and loot boxes

Investigate how variable rewards and in-app purchases monetize impulsivity, especially among minors. Separate regulated gambling, game mechanics, and ordinary entertainment purchases.

### Marriage, divorce, and household duplication

The existence of legal, housing, and consumer spending after divorce does not prove that an “industry” causes marital breakdown. A defensible episode would examine particular legal incentives, advertising practices, or policy structures while respecting the many reasons people leave marriages, including abuse.

### Commercial holidays

Research how advertising, retail calendars, consumer credit, and gift expectations alter particular religious or civic observances. Distinguish voluntary celebration and generosity from coercive status competition.
